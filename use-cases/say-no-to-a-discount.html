How to politely say no to a customer's discount request

Short answer: Thank them, say plainly that your price stays, and offer two or three smaller options at your normal rates, such as fewer rooms, a shorter service or a phased job. Write your discount rule down first. Use AI to draft one reply from your own prices, check every number, then save it as a template.

How to turn down a discount request without losing the customer: offer less scope, not less price. Free AI prompt, example reply and watch-outs. Who it's for: Any owner who quotes a price and hears "Can you do any better?": contractors, cleaners, salons, photographers, caterers, shops and service firms. Saves: a fresh, awkward reply every time (rough estimate)

The situation today

  • A customer asks for a lower price, and the easiest way to end the awkward moment is to knock something off. Then the next customer hears about it.
  • You're not alone in holding prices. In Gusto's survey of 1,731 U.S. owners with fewer than 25 employees, 52% raised prices over the past 12 months, 42% held them steady and 3% cut them.
  • Rough estimate: writing a good reply from scratch takes 10 to 15 minutes each time. A saved template takes a minute to adjust.

Time figures marked "rough estimate" are our estimates, not survey data.

The AI-assisted workflow

Step by step

  1. Write your discount rule in one line, e.g. "No discounts on labor; 5% off when a maintenance plan is prepaid." If the honest rule is "none", write that.
  2. List two or three smaller options at your normal rates: fewer rooms or items, a shorter or basic service, an off-peak time, or the job split into two phases. Put a price next to each.
  3. Write one or two honest reasons your price is what it is, in your own words. Skip this if you'd rather not explain.
  4. Paste the customer's request (names and contact details removed), your rule, your options and your reasons into the prompt below.
  5. Check every price and option against your price list, change anything that doesn't sound like you, and send it.
  6. Save the final reply as a template in your phone, email or booking app so the next request takes a minute.

Copy-paste prompt

I run a [type of business]. A customer asked for a discount. Write a short, friendly reply that keeps my price but keeps the customer.
Use only the facts below. Don't invent prices, discounts, deadlines, reasons, guarantees or policies. If something is missing, write [CHECK].
- What they asked: [paste their message, names and contact details removed]
- What I quoted: [service and price]
- My discount rule: [e.g. "no discounts on labor" or "none"]
- Smaller options at my normal rates: [2 or 3 options, each with its price]
- Why my price is what it is, in my words (optional): [1 or 2 reasons]
- How they reached me: [text / email / in person]

Write: 1) a text or email under 120 words: thank them, say plainly that the price stays, offer the smaller options with their prices, and end with one easy next step; 2) a three-line version I can say on the phone or at the counter; 3) one short follow-up message if they don't reply in a few days. Warm and confident. No apologizing, no guilt, no fake deadlines, no pushy sales lines.

Works in any AI chat assistant. Replace everything in [brackets].

Optional tool

No new tool needed. Draft with any free AI chat assistant, then save the reply as a template in the email, text or booking app you already use.

Watch-outs

  • Keep customer details out of AI chats. Paste what they asked for and your own prices, never names, phone numbers, addresses or payment details.
  • Check every number. AI can invent a discount, a deadline or a "special price" you never offered. Delete anything that isn't on your price list.
  • Don't make up a "regular price" to make a deal look bigger. If you advertise a "was $X, now $Y" price, the FTC's Guides Against Deceptive Pricing say the former price should be one you actually offered openly, in good faith, for a reasonably substantial period.
  • Be consistent. Decide your rule once and apply it to everyone, so one customer's deal doesn't turn into everyone's price. If a contract or written quote already sets the price, check it before you change anything, and ask your attorney if you're unsure.

When not to use AI

  • A customer who is upset about a mistake or a problem with the job. That's a complaint, not a price request; talk to them first.
  • Hardship situations where you choose to help someone. Make that call yourself, in person, not with a template.

Quick answers

How do I politely refuse a customer who wants a discount?

Thank them, say plainly that your price stays, and offer a smaller option at your normal rate, such as fewer items, a shorter service or a phased job. End with one easy next step.

Should I ever give a discount?

Only under a rule you've written down and apply to everyone, such as a prepaid plan. Otherwise, offer less scope rather than a lower price.
